Heuristics and Strategies in Philosophy of Physics

105 recurring strategies extracted from 50 BJPS philosophy-of-physics papers (2016–2026), with AI assistance.

The survey

The survey reads full texts and abstracts of 50 BJPS philosophy-of-physics papers from 2016 to 2026 and extracts the reusable moves papers are built from. Each strategy is scored from 1 to 5 on three axes:

  • Creativity (C): how much new philosophical content the move produces. 5 means seeing something nobody saw; 1 means filling in a template.
  • Usefulness (U): how often opportunities arise and how reliably they turn into publishable work.
  • Mechanisability (M): how well a language model with a corpus and search could run the move to generate candidate ideas (not finished papers).

The scores are editorial judgements, not measurements, and so is the grouping by operation and altitude.

Main findings

The axes pull in the dull direction. Creativity correlates negatively with usefulness (−0.43) and with mechanisability (−0.40); usefulness and mechanisability go together (+0.44). On average, what a model can run is neither very creative nor hard to execute. A generator optimised for "would this be publishable" converges there and produces correct, well-placed, inert papers.

The exceptions are the interesting part. Sixteen strategies score 5 on creativity, and most of them are hard to mechanise. Three are not: rejected-branch retrieval, motivating-case failure and complement construction. In each, the step that looks creative is really a search nobody has run: over options authors explicitly set aside, over the scientific cases a programme cites as its own vindication, over the preconditions of canonical objections.

The blind spot is about the operation, not the level. Debates' option spaces (scorecards, taxonomies) are among the easiest targets for a model; the hard level is the field itself (agenda, practice, institutions). Operation predicts better: occupying and splitting an already-mapped option space is the most mechanisable, while constituting something new is the least.

More than half are highly mechanisable. 55 of the 105 strategies score 4 or 5 on mechanisability — a language model with a corpus and search could plausibly run the move to generate candidate ideas. 16 score the maximum (M=5): these are almost entirely search or gap-finding moves. The remaining 50 require genuine philosophical judgement — recognising what is load-bearing, what counts as a good explanation, what the field actually needs.

One strategy is demonstrably new. Pointing philosophy's apparatus at large language models has essentially no instances before 2024 and dozens afterwards.

Five most mechanisable strategies

The strategies below score M=5 and are ranked by creativity. Each represents a move a language model could plausibly run as a search over a corpus — the creative work is finding the right gap or structural match, not generating genuinely new philosophical content.

Rejected-branch retrieval M5 · C5 · U3
Occupy · Option space

Defend the interpretation the original author explicitly considered and discarded.

What it delivers

A thesis, but only after step 6. Stopping at step 5 yields 'here is a view nobody holds', which is a curiosity.

Example papers
  • Baptiste Le Bihan, The Great Loop: From Conformal Cyclic Cosmology to Aeon Monism (2026)
  • Jake Nebel, A Risky Problem for Permutation Invariance (2026)
  • Jacob A. Barandes, Historical Debates over the Physical Reality of the Wave Function (2026)
Cross-field programme import M5 · C4 · U4
Relocate · Option space

Bring a live research programme from another subfield and show it dissolves an impasse.

What it delivers

A thesis, and a bridge between literatures that others can cross.

Example papers
  • Tushar Menon, Investigating Quantum Mechanics (2026)
  • Daniel Greco, On the Very Idea of an Epistemic Dilemma (2026)
  • Enrico Cinti, Mariani et al., The Unbearable Indefiniteness of Spacetime (2025)
Argument-isomorphism audit M5 · C4 · U3
Audit · Argument

Ask whether two arguments in different debates are structurally the same argument.

What it delivers

A thesis, and a transfer: objections to one now apply to the other.

Example papers
  • Cheng, James Read, The Hole Argument and Putnam's Paradox (2026)
  • Michels, From Intersubjective Correspondence to the Counterpart Relation (2026)
  • Jacobs, Caspar, Quality and Isomorphism: the Model Is Not the Territory (2026)
Hub-and-spoke piping M5 · C3 · U5
Occupy · Option space

Take one physical result and pipe it into every metaphysical debate downstream of it — one paper per spoke.

What it delivers

A programme. Each spoke is a thesis; the hub is the asset.

Example papers
  • Jacob A. Barandes, A Deflationary Account of Quantum Theory and Its Implications for the Complex Numbers (2026)
  • Jørn Kløvfjell Mjelva, Josh Quirke et al., Open Systems as Metaphysically Fundamental: Some Questions (2026)
  • Albert, David, Quantum Mechanics Without a Wave-Function (2026)
Definite-article pluralisation M5 · C3 · U4
Split · Vocabulary

A field refers to something in the singular — the law, the problem, the argument. Show there are several.

What it delivers

A thesis, and a vocabulary correction the field has to adopt.

Example papers
  • Vanessa Seifert, The Many Laws of the Periodic Table (2025)
  • Emily Adlam, Jacob A. Barandes, Against Many Worlds (2026)
  • Jørn Kløvfjell Mjelva, Josh Quirke et al., Open Systems as Metaphysically Fundamental: Some Questions (2026)

Five least mechanisable strategies

The strategies below scored M=1 (or M=2 with C=5) — meaning the key move requires judgement, timing, or personal history that a language model running a corpus search cannot replicate. The M=1 strategies in particular depend on factors entirely outside the text: who you are, what you have already published, and whether you are willing to run an experiment or organise a field.

Auto-critique reversal M1 · C4 · U1
Break · Claim

Rebut a charge you yourself made in earlier work.

What it delivers

A thesis, usually narrow, with unusual credibility attached because of its source.

Run the experiment yourself M1 · C4 · U3
Relocate · Field

Stop arguing about what people judge and go measure it, to the target discipline’s standards.

What it delivers

A thesis with data behind it, and standing in two fields.

Example papers
  • Xiang, Kevin Dorst et al., On the Robustness and Provenance of the Gambler’s Fallacy (2024)
  • Natalja Deng, Latham et al., There’s No Time Like the Present (2024)
  • Mike D. Schneider, Popularity Feedback Constrains Innovation in Cultural Markets (2026)
Publication-ecology change M1 · C3 · U4
Constitute · Field

Change where and with whom you publish, rather than what you do.

What it delivers

Reach. It is not a paper-production strategy so much as a strategy about paper production, which is why it sits oddly in the survey and is included for completeness.

Field constitution M1 · C3 · U3
Constitute · Field

Notice an under-theorised area, then organise a community around it rather than writing the paper yourself.

What it delivers

A field. The slowest and most consequential deliverable in the survey.

Reconciliation by presupposition rejection M2 · C5 · U2
Break · Commitments

Reject a presupposition all parties share; the rival positions turn out to be compatible.

What it delivers

A thesis of an unusual kind — a dissolution that leaves both parties standing.

Example papers
  • Tushar Menon, Investigating Quantum Mechanics (2026)
  • Hans Halvorson, To be a realist about quantum theory (2019)
  • Claudio Calosi, Composition, identity, and emergence (2016)

How the papers were selected

All OpenAlex "article" records from BJPS, 2016–2026 (565 papers) were screened in two stages: first by Claude Haiku on title and abstract (philosophy of physics? research article?), then by Claude Sonnet on the full abstract, retaining only clearly conceptual or philosophical papers (128 eligible). A seeded random draw balanced over three periods (2016–19, 2020–23, 2024–26), with at most two papers per first author per period. Only papers with a findable open copy (publisher OA, arXiv, institutional repository) were kept; 73 drawn papers had no open copy and were dropped. The 50 papers are effectively all BJPS philosophy-of-physics papers from 2016–2026 with a freely accessible full text.
