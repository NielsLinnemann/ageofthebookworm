What Philosophy of Physics Is About

A map of the questions the field asks: seven kinds of question, 43 themes, the typical questions under each, how they spread across the areas of physics, and where the field ends and its neighbours begin. Built from 111 standard resources on the field with the help of language models.

How much of this is AI. The study combined language-model work with human judgement. Gemini 4 Argon (Google) read the resources and extracted the topics, then sorted every topic into the scheme. Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic) and GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI) each proposed an alternative scheme and criticised the draft. The study's author drafted and revised the categories, read the three introductions to the field in full, and labelled the test texts. The checklist was tested with Gemini 4 Argon and Claude Sonnet 5.5. This web page, including the plain-language wording and the placement of the 50 BJPS papers into themes, was prepared by Claude Opus (Anthropic). All assignments may be wrong; treat the map as a starting point, not a verdict.

Main findings

The field sorts best by kind of question, not by area of physics. Seven kinds of question cover all 688 topics extracted from the resources: what a theory's mathematics says about the world; what exists according to a theory; how theories at different levels or scales relate; what physics says about laws, chance, causation and time; what its basic concepts and principles mean; how physical theories are tested and chosen; and which traditional questions of metaphysics physics can answer or reshape.

The kinds of question are not areas of physics in disguise. Most kinds of question turn up in most areas, from classical mechanics to cosmology (see the table below). Quantum mechanics dominates the counts, with 273 of the topics touching it, but this partly reflects the collection: 21 of its 41 encyclopedia entries are about quantum theory.

The borders are graded, not sharp. The three introductions to the field in the collection agree that philosophy of physics is continuous with physics, metaphysics, history and mathematics. What separates it is the main aim of a question: a claim about what a theory means, presupposes or commits us to, rather than a new derivation, a general thesis that uses physics only as an illustration, or a historical account.

A short checklist can tell philosophy of physics from its neighbours fairly reliably. On 38 new test texts, the two language models agreed with the author's own classification in 31 and 32 cases. Clear cases were almost never misclassified; the disagreements were mostly at the borders with metaphysics and with history.

The sorting is fairly stable. When all topics were sorted again after the scheme was revised, 622 of 688 kept the same main theme.

The seven kinds of question

Each kind of question comes with what it is not merely, which marks where a neighbouring field takes over. When a text touches several, it is placed under the question it mainly tries to answer. The measurement problem, symmetry and the direction of time deliberately run through several categories.

Interpretation and representation
159 topics from 60 resources · 12 of the 50 BJPS papers

What a theory's formalism says about the world, and which parts of it represent anything.

Not merely

Not merely solving the equations or computing predictions (physics), and not merely asking what representation is in general (general philosophy of science).

Ontology and fundamentality
112 topics from 59 resources · 5 of the 50 BJPS papers

What exists, and what is fundamental, if a given physical theory is true.

Not merely

Not merely listing the particles of the Standard Model (physics), and not ontology argued from intuition or logic alone (metaphysics proper).

Relations between theories, scales and regimes
100 topics from 50 resources · 9 of the 50 BJPS papers

How theories at different levels, scales or regimes connect: reduction, limits, emergence, compatibility.

Not merely

Not merely deriving one equation from another in a limit (physics), and not reduction and emergence discussed in the abstract or for mind and biology (general philosophy of science, philosophy of mind).

Laws, modality, probability and causation in physics
107 topics from 51 resources · 12 of the 50 BJPS papers

What physics says about necessity and possibility, chance, determinism, causation and the direction of time.

Not merely

Not merely calculating probabilities or checking well-posedness (physics), and not a general theory of laws, chance or causation built on everyday examples (metaphysics proper).

Conceptual foundations: concepts, principles, theorems and scrutiny
109 topics from 50 resources · 7 of the 50 BJPS papers

Clarifying, reconstructing and scrutinising the basic concepts, principles, theorems and claims of physics.

Not merely

Not merely proving the theorem or applying the principle (physics, mathematical physics), and not conceptual analysis of ordinary language (general philosophy).

Evidence, explanation and method in physics
71 topics from 36 resources · 3 of the 50 BJPS papers

How physical theories are tested, chosen, used to explain, and built.

Not merely

Not merely doing the experiment or the fit (physics), and not confirmation, explanation or modelling discussed without engaging a particular physical case (general philosophy of science).

Metaphysics constrained by physics
30 topics from 22 resources · 2 of the 50 BJPS papers

Traditional metaphysical questions answered or reshaped by what physical theories say.

Not merely

Not metaphysics that only mentions physics as colour or uses outdated physics as a premise; it must let the structure of a theory constrain the answer.

Two directions

Across all seven categories, work can start from either side (Wüthrich and Barandes both draw this distinction, with different names):

  • From philosophy to physics. A question that began in philosophy (determinism, what exists, causation, time, identity) is brought to a physical theory and answered with its resources, sharpening the philosophical concept on the way. Wüthrich: philosophy of physics in the narrower sense; Barandes: physical philosophy.
  • From physics to philosophy. A problem that arises inside physics (measurement, the second law, the hole argument, assessing quantum gravity programmes) is treated with philosophical methods: analysis of concepts, premises and arguments. Wüthrich: foundations of physics; Barandes: philosophical physics.

Most good work moves back and forth between the two; the directions are starting points, not separate fields.

Ways of working

These are approaches that can serve any kind of question. They are not categories of their own: a historical study or a thought experiment counts as philosophy of physics only if it serves one of the seven kinds of question.

  • Rigorous scrutiny. Slowing down over a successful theory or a confident claim to find gaps in definitions, hidden premises, conflated concepts, missing categories and parts that do not fit (Barandes). The checklist uses it as the mark of work done well.
  • Historical-conceptual reconstruction. Going back to how a concept or argument arose (Newton's bucket, the Leibniz-Clarke exchange, Einstein's hole argument, Boltzmann's shifting views, Bohr) to clarify what is at stake now. It counts as philosophy of physics when the history serves a conceptual or interpretive point; establishing who believed what is history of physics.
  • Formal reconstruction. Rewriting a theory in a cleaner mathematical setting (coordinate-free geometry, algebraic or axiomatic frameworks, state-space formulations) to see what it presupposes.
  • Theorem analysis. Stating the premises of a no-go or limiting result exactly and asking which premise carries physical or metaphysical weight.
  • Thought experiments and toy models. Buckets, holes, demons, Wigner's friend, time machines, deliberately impossible models used to isolate which principle does the work.
  • Case studies of practice. Looking at how physicists actually calculate, approximate and model (renormalization, perturbation theory, hydrodynamics, simulations) rather than only at idealized textbook theories.
  • Constructive contribution. Using philosophical analysis to help build or assess new physics, notably in quantum gravity, quantum foundations and relational mechanics. Barandes and Wüthrich count this as central; Read puts the emphasis on understanding existing theories.
  • Criticism and appreciation. Treating theories as creative achievements to be understood, criticised and appreciated for their structure, much as a critic treats works of art (Read).

Where the field ends

Each line below is built first from the three introductions (Wüthrich, Barandes, Read) and then checked against the border remarks gathered from the rest of the collection. The introductions agree that the borders are porous: none treats philosophy of physics as sealed off from physics, metaphysics, history or mathematics. The lines are therefore tests for the main aim of a question, not for who wrote it or where it appeared.

Physics proper

Physics builds and tests theories and derives results within them. Philosophy of physics asks what theories say, presuppose and commit us to, whether their claims and arguments hold together, and how they connect to the world; it starts where mathematics and data no longer settle the question. It may serve new physics (by scrutiny of concepts and premises), but its own deliverable is that analysis, not a new derivation or measurement.

Counts as philosophy of physics if

the output is a claim about what a theory means, presupposes or commits us to, about whether a physical claim or argument is sound, or about how formalism connects to the world.

Not if it is merely

a new derivation, prediction, model or measurement, even a conceptually motivated one.

Where the three introductions differ

Whether helping to build new physics is a central aim (Barandes, Wüthrich) or a side benefit (Read). Because the line is graded (Wüthrich), texts whose main output is physics but which argue a conceptual point are scored borderline, not rejected.

Metaphysics proper

Metaphysics asks about time, identity, causation, modality and composition in general. All three introductions count metaphysics informed by physics as part of the field; the border runs where physics stops constraining the answer. A question is philosophy of physics when a specific theory's structure decides or reshapes the answer, and when the metaphysical concept is sharpened in contact with the theory.

Counts as philosophy of physics if

the metaphysical thesis is stated in terms of a theory's structures (state spaces, spacetime geometry, symmetries) and tested against them.

Not if it is merely

metaphysics that mentions physics as colour, relies on slogans or a folk picture of particles, or argues a priori about what lies beyond any empirical access.

Where the three introductions differ

How much of the field is metaphysics: the core for Wüthrich, one strand of three for Read, one mode of two for Barandes.

General philosophy of science

General philosophy of science studies confirmation, explanation, reduction, realism and models across the sciences. Philosophy of physics asks these questions where the answer turns on features peculiar to physics: a unique universe, theories without data, empirically equivalent interpretations, renormalization, infinite limits, gauge redundancy. Physics cases are then the subject, not illustrations.

Counts as philosophy of physics if

the epistemic or methodological claim depends on the specific situation of a physical theory or practice.

Not if it is merely

a general account of confirmation, explanation or causation that uses a physics example only as an illustration, or none at all.

Where the three introductions differ

How central method is: less central than physics and metaphysics for Wüthrich, a further mode for Barandes, a full category in this map.

History of physics

History reconstructs what was done, believed and argued, and why, in its context. Philosophy of physics uses history to understand a theory's content and to clarify concepts and arguments still at stake (absolute motion, general covariance, Copenhagen, Boltzmann). The introductions differ on how sharp this border is.

Counts as philosophy of physics if

the historical material serves a conceptual, interpretive or methodological conclusion about physics.

Not if it is merely

establishing who believed what, when and why.

Where the three introductions differ

Barandes would count history of physics itself as part of the field; Read and Wüthrich treat it as continuous but distinct. This map follows the narrower reading, so purely historical work is scored as a neighbour.

Philosophy of mathematics and mathematical physics

Mathematical physics proves theorems about the structures used in physics; philosophy of mathematics asks about mathematics as such. Philosophy of physics needs both but asks a further question: which mathematical structure represents what in the world, and what a theorem shows once its premises are given a physical reading.

Counts as philosophy of physics if

the question concerns the physical reading of a mathematical structure or the physical significance of a theorem.

Not if it is merely

proving the theorem, generalizing the structure, or asking about mathematical objects in general.

Where the three introductions differ

Read stresses continuity with mathematics; Barandes stresses the gap between mathematical and philosophical rigour. Both fit the test: mathematics is necessary, the physical reading is what makes it philosophy of physics.

Further away: philosophy of mind (consciousness in collapse, personal identity under branching) and philosophy of religion (fine-tuning and design) are reached through physics but continue beyond it; the collection treats these as places where philosophy of physics hands over. Sociology of physics, which Barandes counts as a mode of the field, is not covered by the collection.

What the three introductions say

Three texts in the collection are about what philosophy of physics is, rather than about one of its topics. They were read in full and used as the main basis for the borders and the checklist. The summaries are the study author's own wording.

Christian Wüthrich, Philosophie der Physik (German introduction, 2015 draft)

Read the text

What the field is

Finds two loose directions in the field. In one, questions that began in philosophy (is the world deterministic, what are its basic constituents, is there a physical counterpart of causation) are brought to physics and answered with its means, which forces the philosophical concepts to be sharpened. In the other, the foundations of physics, problems that arise inside physics (the measurement problem, recovering the second law from statistical physics, the Leibniz-Newton dispute) are treated with broadly philosophical methods.

Versus physics

The two directions are modes or starting points, not separate fields. Good work moves back and forth, and a principled line between physics and philosophy cannot be drawn; the difference is at most one of degree.

Versus metaphysics

Metaphysical, especially ontological, questions are at the core of the field, not a neighbour; but they must be answered with the resources of physics.

Versus general philosophy of science

Methodological and epistemic questions are real but, in its view, less central than the physical and metaphysical ones; current physics gives philosophers of science plenty to do and may even need them.

Versus history

Physics was part of natural philosophy until roughly Kant; an independent philosophy of physics appears with Kant or with logical positivism, to which the present field remains closely tied. Historical debates are used as examples of foundational dispute.

Versus mathematics

Progress often comes from the interplay of physics, mathematics and philosophy; interpretations of theories without a solid mathematical formulation (most field theories) should be treated with caution.

What good work looks like

Sharpen concepts until the question becomes answerable; state problems so they become inescapable (the measurement problem); keep conclusions provisional where the physics is unsettled; be explicit that a problem like measurement arises only if the theory is read realistically.

Jacob A. Barandes, Rigor in Philosophy of Physics

Read the text

What the field is

Defines the field broadly as inquiry at the intersections of philosophy and physics, with two main modes: physical philosophy (metaphysics, epistemology and philosophy of science kept in line with our best physics and mathematics) and philosophical physics (using the tools of analytic philosophy to make progress on open problems in physics, in the way theoretical or mathematical physics use theirs). It also counts the history, sociology and methodology of physics as further modes.

Versus physics

Philosophical rigour is needed exactly where mathematical symbols and experimental data run out: in connecting formalism to reality and in extending theories to new regimes. Physicists often speculate freely and contradict each other in slogans; the philosopher's contribution is careful scrutiny, which slows down rather than building models, yet can open new physics (Einstein's scrutiny of inertial frames is the model case).

Versus metaphysics

Physics-informed metaphysics belongs to the field (physical philosophy), but claims must be consistent with what theories actually say; asserting facts beyond any empirical access, such as a top-level system containing everything, is a priori metaphysics and should make one suspicious.

Versus general philosophy of science

Philosophy of science informed by physics is part of physical philosophy; general standards of philosophical rigour apply first (explicit argument types, sharp ingredients, known limits of sharpness).

Versus history

History and sociology of physics are counted as modes of the field; historical examples are one tool of scrutiny.

Versus mathematics

Mathematical rigour is not enough: axioms of probability do not say what a probability is, and a theorem can be valid as mathematics yet fail as a physical theorem if its premises or conclusions are not connected to the right physical or metaphysical notions ('connection problems').

What good work looks like

Deep training in the relevant physics and mathematics; independence from folklore; arguments of an explicit type with sharply stated definitions and premises; knowing and explaining the limits of one's precision; looking for gaps, hidden premises, conflated concepts, missing categories and structures that do not fit.

James Read, Why philosophy of physics? (Aeon essay)

Read the text

What the field is

The systematic study of our best physical theories, from Newtonian mechanics to relativity, quantum theory, the Standard Model and cosmology, superseded or not.

Versus physics

Continuous with physics; the difference is one of emphasis. Philosophers aim to understand the structure and the consequences for reality of theories we already have; physicists build new theories and test them. Philosophy of physics can study theories without the pressure to produce new ones.

Versus metaphysics

Continuous with philosophy proper, because what a theory says about time or identity cannot be judged without the long philosophical scrutiny of those notions.

Versus general philosophy of science

Not discussed as a separate border.

Versus history

Continuous with history, because understanding a theory's content often requires knowing how it was developed and with what tools; the field is as old as physics (Newton, Leibniz-Clarke, the problem of space, Mach, Einstein).

Versus mathematics

Continuous with mathematics, because a full understanding of a theory often requires settling subtle mathematical points.

What good work looks like

Command of physics, philosophy, mathematics and history; dialogue with practising physicists. It also likens the field to art criticism: theories are creative achievements open to critical and aesthetic appreciation, not only to analysis.

Where they disagree

  • Building new physics. Barandes counts using philosophy to make progress on open problems of physics as one of the field's two main modes, and Wüthrich counts the foundations of physics, where the questions are physical ones. Read puts the emphasis on understanding theories we already have and leaves building and testing new ones to physicists. The map keeps theory-building contributions inside the field (constructive contribution, theory choice) but the checklist still asks for a conceptual payoff.
  • Where the line with physics runs. Wüthrich denies that a principled line exists (only degrees); Read calls the difference one of emphasis; Barandes locates the field's own kind of rigour where mathematics and data run out. The checklist uses Barandes's location as its test and Wüthrich's point as the reason for a borderline verdict.
  • Metaphysics: core or neighbour. Wüthrich places ontology at the centre; Read lists implications for classical philosophical topics as one of three kinds of question; Barandes includes physics-informed metaphysics but warns against a priori metaphysics. None treats metaphysics as outside the field. The map's category 'metaphysics constrained by physics' is therefore relabelled as part of the core, with the border drawn where physics stops constraining the answer.
  • How central method is. Wüthrich calls epistemic and methodological questions less central; Barandes sets methodology aside as a further mode; Read's only example is underdetermination in cosmology. The map gives evidence, explanation and method a full category, because the handbooks devote whole parts to explanation, confirmation and cosmology; the counts (fewest topics after the metaphysics category) agree that it is smaller.
  • History and sociology. Barandes counts history and sociology of physics as modes of the field; Read calls the field continuous with history; Wüthrich uses history as background. The map treats history as a neighbour whose material serves conceptual points; sociology of physics is not covered by the collection and is left out, which Barandes would count as a gap.
  • Appreciation. Only Read treats aesthetic and critical appreciation of theories as an aim. The map adds it as a way of working and as a theory-choice question, not as a category.

A checklist for telling philosophy of physics from its neighbours

The checklist was written so that a language model can apply it to a draft or a research question. The first four questions decide whether a text belongs to the field; the fifth says whether it is done well.

  1. Physics anchor. Does the text engage the actual content of at least one physical theory, model, result or practice (its formalism, principles, theorems, approximations or use), at a level consistent with what the theory says, rather than only naming it or repeating slogans about it?
  2. Philosophical question. Is the main question one that calculation, derivation or experiment within the theory cannot settle on its own? It may start from philosophy (a question about determinism, existence, causation, time or identity brought to physics) or from physics (a problem inside physics treated by analysing concepts, premises and arguments). It should fall under at least one of the seven categories.
  3. Two-way dependence. Would the answer change if the physics were different, and is the payoff a claim about what the theory says, commits us to, how its claims connect to the world, or how it is justified (rather than a new physical result)?
  4. Not merely a neighbour. Does it fail by being merely: (a) physics: a new derivation, model, prediction or measurement; (b) metaphysics or general philosophy of science: physics used only as illustration or colour, so the argument would go through with any example, or a priori claims beyond any physical constraint; (c) history: mainly who believed what and why; (d) mathematics: a theorem about structures with no claim about what they represent?
  5. Done well (does not decide membership). Are definitions and premises stated sharply, the link between mathematical items and physical or metaphysical notions made explicit, and the limits of the argument's precision acknowledged?

Scoring

Each of the first four questions is scored 0, 1 or 2, for a total out of 8. A score of 7 or 8 means philosophy of physics; 4 to 6 means a borderline case, to be reported with the neighbouring field it leans towards; 0 to 3 means it belongs to another field. A zero on any single question limits the verdict to borderline. Rigour is scored separately, because it says whether the work is done well, not whether it belongs to the field. When the main result of a text is a derivation, theorem, model or physical argument, it counts as borderline unless it also argues for a conceptual claim.

  • Physics engagement
    • 0: physics absent or only named
    • 1: physics used as an illustration, as a slogan, or at textbook-summary level
    • 2: works with the theory's actual structure (equations, models, theorems, practice)
  • Philosophical question
    • 0: none; the aim is a result, a description or a narrative
    • 1: a philosophical remark, but secondary to another aim
    • 2: the main aim is a question of one of the seven kinds, from either direction
  • Dependence and payoff
    • 0: the answer would be the same whatever physics says, or the payoff is a physical result
    • 1: physics constrains the answer only loosely, or the main deliverable is a derivation, theorem, model or physical argument with a conceptual claim attached
    • 2: the answer turns on specific physics and the payoff is a claim about meaning, commitment, connection to the world, status or justification
  • Distinct from neighbours
    • 0: clearly a neighbour's work (see Q4)
    • 1: borderline with a named neighbour
    • 2: clearly not reducible to a neighbour
  • Rigour (reported separately)
    • 0: slogans, undefined terms, formalism and world run together
    • 1: mostly clear, some steps or connections left implicit
    • 2: sharp definitions and premises, explicit connection between formalism and world, limits stated

Limits of this test

  • One annotator (the study's author), abstracts only; agreement with the author's labels is not ground truth, and several held-out errors are cases where the author's label is itself debatable.
  • The tie-breaker was added after seeing errors on the old set, so the old-set figures from then on are optimistic; the held-out set is the fairer measure.
  • Clear philosophy-of-physics texts mostly score 8 of 8, so the score separates the field from its neighbours but says little about degrees within it.
  • The rigour score is reported by the models but not evaluated here.

How the study was made

  • Sources. The philosophy-of-physics section of a shared PDF library, collected from a German materials page for the field, plus 41 Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries and the openly available chapters of two handbooks (41 of 54 chapters of the Routledge Companion, 13 of 17 chapters of the Oxford Handbook). 111 files in all; 107 had readable content. The rest are contents lists and source pages, used only for the resource list.
  • Extraction. Long texts were shortened to their first and last parts plus all section headings. For each resource, Gemini 4 Argon listed three to ten topics in its own words, with their central questions, the physics involved, why the question is not just physics or just metaphysics, and remarks on the field's borders. This gave 688 topics and 388 remarks on borders.
  • Scheme. The study's author drafted the categories from the topics and remarks. Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol each proposed an independent scheme, both arriving at similar kinds of question, and then criticised the draft, which was revised in response.
  • Sorting. Gemini 4 Argon gave each topic a main theme, an optional second theme and up to three areas of physics. In a hand check of 40 random topics, the author agreed with the main theme in about 37.
  • Second version. The borders and the checklist were rebuilt from the three introductions to the field, the scheme was checked against them, and all topics were sorted again.
  • BJPS papers. The 50 BJPS philosophy-of-physics papers (2016–2026) from the companion study of research strategies were placed by Claude Opus, one theme per paper, on the basis of title and content.
  • Cost. About $9.10 in language-model calls for the original study, under a hard limit of $20.

The scheme is one reasonable way of dividing the field, not the only one. Counts depend on the collection and on one model's sorting.
